\#\# Problem and Context

Currently, our team has several major decisions coming up for our Pharmacy Website Game. While some of my teammates are focusing on backend development, I want to explore the more open-ended concepts of our project through brainstorming and discussion. Specifically, I want to address what our actual game is going to be. I know that our partner wants some kind of matching game, but that description is too vague to give our team a clear direction for development.

Deciding on the type of matching game is important because it will serve as the foundation for our website. The gameplay will influence how our backend systems are used, how information is stored and processed, and how the frontend is designed. For example, different types of matching games may require different rules, data structures, user interactions, and scoring systems. Establishing the overall concept early will help us determine which features we need and how the different parts of our website should work together. We could decide later to implement both, but to give us direction we should at least come to a consensus about our first game design so we can get the basics.

If we do not make this decision soon, we risk developing features without a shared understanding of the intended gameplay. This could lead to conflicting assumptions, unnecessary rework, and difficulties integrating the frontend and backend as development progresses. By discussing the available options now, we can establish a shared vision for the game before committing too much time to a particular implementation. 

\#\# Options

\#\#\# Option A: \[Memory Matching\]

Description:

Players flip over cards to find matching pairs of information. For example, one card could display a generic drug name while another displays its corresponding trade name. Could also be where the card shows up for a split second, disappears, and then they have to match it to the correct card. 

Benefits:  
From an actual game standpoint, it puts more pressure on the user as they need to react quicker, making the game have higher stakes. Personally, I think it would be more fun this way, as it would feel less like just normal flashcards.

Costs: 

The game may focus more on memory than on understanding pharmacy information. The team would need to decide which fields form matching pairs and ensure that each pair has a clear relationship. The frontend would also need to manage card states, matched pairs, and incorrect selections. It can also be kind of limited in the different ways we could adapt it to make new game modes. 

\#\#\# Option B: \[Normal Matching\]

Description: 3 cards appear at the top of the screen. There will be a huge pile of cards, with only a few matching to each of the 3 cards. For example, 1 of the 3 cards: generic name, with 3 cards in the pile that match it with trade name, mechanism of action, and disease use.

Benefits: More open ended, so you can adapt it slightly and have different game modes. Unlike with memory matching you can also give feedback on incorrect answers, as with memory matching you could make tons of matches, especially in a desperate attempt to get them right. 

Costs: Could be less interesting from a gameplay standpoint, as it would be much slower. 

\#\#\# Option C: Do Nothing

Without making a decision on this we could start working on pieces that won’t match up, or play together cohesively. We basically won’t have any common ground about what core mechanics should do. This intune could lead to delays, or a messy project. It can also make it harder for us to work as a team, as it could be difficult to separate work into more manageable chunks. 

\#\# Analysis

There isn’t a ton of technical analysis that I can do, as they’re just concepts for now and we haven’t decided on coding systems (databases, languages), that I would need to give examples of how they would be implemented, and how they would compare. Instead, I am going to give the differences in their example implementations.

Memory Matching: Scoring based on timing, or based on the number of matches a player gets wrong. Checking answers only involves one check, that being its pair. The check happens instantly after the player attempts to match 2\. This means testing the first initial creation of it would be very easy, as you could have a test where it just shows 4 cards, and flags them either as matching or not. However, as brought up in the costs section in options, in actual gameplay from a studying standpoint, it relies a bit on reaction time, and not just solely on knowledge. 

Normal Matching: Scoring based on the number of correct pairs. Checking wrong answers would take multiple checks, as it would have multiple answers. Check happens once, after putting what they think are the correct sets under 1 of the 3\. 

\#\# Recommendation and Verification Plan

Normal matching would be better to start with as it can be much more easily expanded on, as since there are more comparisons (multiple different answers) you can mix and match the ways you check this. The scoring system is easier to count, as it could be as simple as 1 point for every correct card under one of the three cards, while the memory matching would be based on time which would be slightly more difficult. An initial build test could look like 1 card with 4 different options, 3 correct 1 incorrect. Text would pop up after the user submits their 3 final answers, saying the number they got correct and highlighting the correct answers in green and the incorrect ones in red. 

Testing could involve things like, trying to assign more than 3 items to one card, preventing duplicate correct answers from popping up, and correct score count/display. There is much more you can test, however these three showcase the core functions of it. The first one makes sure the cards can be properly interacted with. The second one shows how it can properly communicate with the database, and pull data from it. The third brings in the actual game portion, of having a score or being able to know if you got the answers right/validation.
